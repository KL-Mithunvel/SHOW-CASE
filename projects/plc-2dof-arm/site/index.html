PLC 2-DOF Robotic Arm
Project case study · Mechatronics & Automation · VIT Chennai

A PLC-controlled 2-DOF robotic arm, designed by simulation

A two-axis pick-and-place arm that lifts a 5 kg ferrous part with an electromagnet, swings it 90° and sets it down. The structure was checked in Ansys before building, those results set the motor sizes, and the arm runs on an industrial PLC with a touch-panel HMI. It has been built and tested on the bench.

Step 1 · Structural simulation

Checking the structure before building it

The question was simple: with 5 kg hanging off a 420 mm arm, does any part bend too much or come close to yielding? The model was built and solved in Ansys Mechanical 2023 R1, first in two held poses (static) and then through a full moving cycle (transient).

Model preparation

Bolts, screws and bearings were removed, small holes and fillets suppressed, and mating parts bonded. These details barely affect overall stiffness but make the mesh slow and messy. The assembly was then merged into five solid bodies.

Materials: steel where it stays still, aluminium where it moves

Mild steel AISI 1010Aluminium 6061
Aluminium 6061 matches the steel's yield strength at about one third of the density, which cuts the gravity torque the shoulder motor must hold. The trade-off is one third of the stiffness, so the links bend the most.

Mesh, supports and loads

Base fixed in all directions, gravity on every body, and the 5 kg payload weight at the magnet.

Two held poses were solved. Pose A (pick-up): arm extended over the part. Pose B (holding): base turned 90° with Link-1 raised, part held. Each solve reports deformation, von Mises stress and factor of safety (yield strength ÷ peak stress; Ansys caps the display at 15).

Step 1 · Results

Stiff, lightly stressed, and the joint governs

The largest deflection anywhere is 0.085 mm at the Link-2 tip, about the width of a hair. Peak stress is under 9 MPa against yield strengths of 250–259 MPa. The joint is the most critical part because it carries the whole cantilever moment of the arm and load.

Step 2 · Actuator selection

From simulated loads to motor choice

The simulation fixed three requirements: the gravity moment of the governing pose, the speed and acceleration of the cycle, and a tip accuracy in line with the 0.085 mm structural deflection. Both axes use integrated harmonic-drive servos, each with motor, drive, absolute encoder and strain-wave reducer in one housing.

Shoulder: M8010, 81:1

The shoulder holds the arm against gravity. Its worst case is Link-1 horizontal at full reach with the part held.

Base: M8025, 101:1

The base turns about a vertical axis, so gravity gives no driving torque and the move needs under 1 N·m. The base was sized for what passes through it: the arm's ~31 N·m overturning moment on the output bearing, with the governing joint (FOS 4.63) mounted on top. The M8025 is rated at 85 N·m continuous and 178 N·m peak (80:1 data), and the 101:1 version still reaches about 15 rpm, 30× the 0.5 rpm the cycle needs.

Step 3 · Control system

Industrial PLC + HMI control

Step 3 · Inside the PLC program

How the ladder program moves the arm

The program is one ladder routine called MAIN, 39 networks long, written in KincoBuilder 8.5 for the K615S-16DT. It does not count pulses. Instead it writes a target position for each joint into the servo drives over CANopen and reads the actual position back, so the PLC is a coordinator and the drives run their own position loops. The two sub-routines SBR_0 and SBR_1 are empty.

Note: the pulse and direction outputs shown in the electrical drawings (Q0.0 to Q0.3) are not used by this saved program. Motion goes over the CAN bus. The magnet output Q0.7 and the e-stop input I0.0 are used as drawn.

Talking to the drives

The PLC is the CANopen master, node 127, at 1000 kbps. The base drive is node 1 and the shoulder drive is node 2. Each drive exchanges three receive PDOs (commands) and up to three transmit PDOs (feedback). The PDO table in KincoBuilder maps each CANopen object to a PLC memory address, so ladder code reads and writes those addresses like any other variable.

CANopen network
  • Baud rate 1000 kbps, SDO timeout 60 ms
  • Configure slaves at start-up, power-on delay 3 s
  • Heartbeat: drives send every 500 ms, PLC accepts up to 600 ms
  • If a node goes silent the PLC stops that node
  • PLC's own CANopen slave function is off
Ethernet and serial
  • PLC address 192.168.0.252, mask 255.255.255.0, gateway 192.168.0.1
  • Modbus TCP on port 502, timeout 2000 ms
  • Port 0 at 115200, ports 1 and 2 (RS485) at 9600, all 8 data bits, no parity, 1 stop bit
  • The HMI must sit on 192.168.0.x with an address other than 252

Drive enable: three controlword values

Networks 0 to 2 and 6 to 8 decide what to write into each drive's controlword. The values follow the standard CiA 402 bit layout.

Holding position and jogging

Each axis has a target position in encoder counts. When the operator holds a jog button, the program writes a fixed target far out in that direction and sets the speed from the HMI. When nothing is requested (networks 4 and 10), the target is simply copied from the actual position, so the drive keeps holding where it stopped. Releasing a button therefore stops the arm without a separate stop command.

Teach, home and the auto cycle

Teaching copies the live positions into stored points: base positions in %VD2300 to %VD2316 and shoulder positions in %VD2400 to %VD2416, four bytes apart, five points each. Homing moves both axes to fixed counts and checks them against thresholds (base below −745000, shoulder above −57648500). The auto cycle only starts when both home flags are set.

Each auto step does the same thing. It loads the taught pair for that point into the two target registers, builds a window of ±2000 counts around each target in %VD3000 to %VD3110, and sets the step's flag when both actual positions are inside their windows. The next step starts from that flag. Pick a step to see which networks and addresses it uses.

Things to check on the program

Overlapping registersThe cycle counter is copied to %VW2400, which is the first two bytes of the shoulder's first taught point %VD2400. Clearing or counting overwrites that point. Moving the counter mirror fixes it.
Nothing is retentiveAll four retentive ranges are set to length 0, so taught points and counters reset at power-off. Teach again after each restart, or move them into the permanent area.
Drive mode is set elsewhereModes of operation (6060) is mapped but never written by the program. The drives must already be in position mode.
Counts are unscaledJog and home constants are raw encoder counts. Recording counts per degree next to them would make the program readable.
HMI screensThe panel project file is encrypted and has not been opened yet, so its screenshots are not included on this page.

Engineering judgement

Next validation steps

The design passes with margin. These are the checks that would take it from "simulated and working" to "validated":

Local joint refinementRe-mesh the joint pin bore finer and confirm the 4.63 FOS with a convergence check.
Bolts and bearingsThey were removed for the global model. A separate hand check of the joint fasteners and bearing would back the "joint is safe" result.
Full time historyPull peak transient values from mid-swing, not only at the end of the cycle.
CAD masses for sizingReplace estimated link masses and read the joint reaction torque straight from Ansys. The shoulder torque has about 1.6× margin on peak.
Supply voltageServos are catalogued at 36 VDC and fed 24 V. Fine for this slow cycle, but worth confirming on the nameplate.
Measure itDial-gauge the tip under 5 kg and compare with the 0.085 mm prediction.
